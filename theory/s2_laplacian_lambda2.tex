\noindent\textbf{Purpose.}
S2 summarises a whole network by one number, the algebraic connectivity
$\lambda_2$ \cite{fiedler}. This note proves the two facts the simulator relies
on ($\lambda_2>0$ iff connected; $\lambda_2\le\frac{N}{N-1}\delta_{\min}$),
states what each verification check tests, shows that in this model a link is
up exactly when it is shorter than a \emph{link range}, and uses that to
explain the two sweeps. Mathematics used: symmetric matrices,
eigenvalues, quadratic forms, the Courant--Fischer characterisation.

\section{From links to a matrix}

Take $N$ nodes. From S1, each pair $(i,j)$ has a bit error rate
$P_b(d_{ij}, J/S, \rho)$; the link is \emph{up} if $P_b\le\BERout$.
Collect the result in the adjacency matrix
\[
  A_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{link } (i,j) \text{ up},\ i\ne j \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\]
symmetric with zero diagonal. The degree $d_i = \sum_j A_{ij}$ counts a node's
live links; $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_N)$. The \emph{graph Laplacian} is
\[
  L = D - A .
\]
Its rows sum to zero by construction: $L\bone = D\bone - A\bone = (d_i) - (d_i) = 0$.

\section{Three facts about $L$}

\paragraph{(a) $L$ is symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real.}
$D$ and $A$ are symmetric. Hence $L$ has $N$ real eigenvalues, which we order
$\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\dots\le\lambda_N$, and an orthonormal eigenbasis.
\texttt{np.linalg.eigvalsh} is the solver for this case (the ``h'' is for Hermitian).

\paragraph{(b) The quadratic form is a sum of squared differences over edges.}
For any vector $\bx\in\mathbb R^N$,
\begin{equation}
  \bx\T L\bx = \sum_i d_i x_i^2 - \sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_ix_j
             = \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i - x_j)^2 \ \ge\ 0 .
  \label{eq:qf}
\end{equation}
To see the second equality, expand $\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(x_i-x_j)^2$: each
edge contributes $x_i^2 + x_j^2$ (which sums to $\sum_i d_ix_i^2$ over all edges
touching $i$) and $-2x_ix_j$ (which sums to $-\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_ix_j$ since each
unordered edge appears twice in the double sum).

\paragraph{(c) $\lambda_1 = 0$ always.}
From (b), $L$ is positive semidefinite, so all $\lambda_k\ge0$; and $L\bone=0$
shows $0$ is attained with eigenvector $\bone$. Thus $\lambda_1=0$ carries no
information about the graph, which is why the second eigenvalue is used.

\section{Theorem: $\lambda_2>0$ if and only if the graph is connected}

\begin{theorem}
The multiplicity of the eigenvalue $0$ of $L$ equals the number of connected
components of the graph. In particular $\lambda_2>0 \iff$ the graph is connected.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $L\bx = 0$. Then $\bx\T L\bx = 0$, and by \eqref{eq:qf} every edge has
$x_i = x_j$. Following edges, $\bx$ is constant on each connected component.
Conversely, any vector constant on components is in the null space (each row
of $L$ only mixes a node with its neighbours, which share its value). So the
null space is spanned by the indicator vectors of the components, and its
dimension is the number of components $c$. Hence $\lambda_1=\dots=\lambda_c=0$
and $\lambda_{c+1}>0$; with $c=1$ this reads $\lambda_2>0$.
\end{proof}

\texttt{VERIFICATION 2} is a test of the implementation, not of
the theorem: for every graph evaluated during the sweeps (87{,}000 evaluations;
many are repeats, because the same placements are thresholded at different
ranges) it computes both $[\lambda_2>10^{-9}]$ and a breadth-first search, and
requires them to agree. Zero mismatches means the adjacency construction, the
Laplacian, and the eigen-solver are consistent with an independent algorithm.
(The $10^{-9}$ tolerance absorbs floating-point round-off; $\lambda_2$ of a
disconnected graph comes back as $\pm10^{-15}$, not exactly $0$.)

\section{Two exact benchmarks}

\begin{proposition}[Complete graph]
For $K_N$ (every pair linked), $\lambda_2 = N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$A = \bone\bone\T - I$ and $D = (N-1)I$, so $L = NI - \bone\bone\T$. On the
subspace $\bx\perp\bone$ we have $\bone\bone\T\bx = 0$, so $L\bx = N\bx$: the
eigenvalue $N$ has multiplicity $N-1$. Together with $\lambda_1=0$ this is the
whole spectrum, and $\lambda_2 = N$.
\end{proof}
\texttt{VERIFICATION 1} checks this for $N=10,20,30$. It catches a wrong sign
convention for $L$ or a wrong eigenvalue index.

\begin{proposition}[Fiedler's degree bound \cite{fiedler}]
Let $\delta_{\min} = \min_i d_i$. Then
$\displaystyle \lambda_2 \le \frac{N}{N-1}\,\delta_{\min}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the Courant--Fischer characterisation,
$\lambda_2 = \min_{\bx\perp\bone,\ \bx\ne0}\ \bx\T L\bx/\bx\T\bx$, so any
particular $\bx\perp\bone$ gives an upper bound. Let $v$ be a node of minimum
degree and take $\bx = e_v - \tfrac1N\bone$ (orthogonal to $\bone$ since its
entries sum to $1-1=0$). Every edge at $v$ contributes $(x_v - x_j)^2 = 1$ and
every other edge contributes $0$, so $\bx\T L\bx = d_v = \delta_{\min}$.
Also $\bx\T\bx = (1-\tfrac1N)^2 + (N-1)\tfrac1{N^2} = \tfrac{N-1}{N}$. Hence
$\lambda_2 \le \delta_{\min}\cdot\tfrac{N}{N-1}$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Correction.} The first draft of the script tested
the bound $\lambda_2\le\delta_{\min}$ and failed on 16{,}195
graphs. The test was wrong, not the code: for $K_N$, $\lambda_2=N$ exceeds
$\delta_{\min}=N-1$. The bound above is the correct one and holds on all
graphs (\texttt{VERIFICATION 3}).

\section{Why $\lambda_2$ and not ``number of live links''}

Two graphs with the same number of links can differ completely: 20 links
scattered evenly leave a network in one piece; 20 links concentrated on the
only path between two clusters, if removed, split it. $\lambda_2$ reflects
that structure. It also has a dynamical meaning: for the consensus dynamics
$\dot{\bx} = -L\bx$ (each node moves toward the average of its neighbours),
the disagreement decays as $e^{-\lambda_2 t}$ \cite{olfati}. A falling
$\lambda_2$ therefore means \emph{slower} coordination before it means
\emph{no} coordination, which a binary connected/disconnected flag cannot show.
For that reason the script records, besides the collapse threshold, the $J/S$
at which the mean $\lambda_2$ has fallen to half its unjammed value (the
``knee'').

\section{Links as a distance threshold}

\paragraph{The link range.}
In this model the jammer is far from all nodes, so every receiver sees the same
$J$, while the received signal falls with link length $d$. Both
$E_b/N_0(d)$ and $E_b/N_J(d)$ decrease with $d$, so the link BER increases with
$d$, and
\[
  P_b(d_{ij})\le\BERout \iff d_{ij}\le r(J/S,\rho),
\]
where the \emph{link range} $r$ solves $P_b(r)=\BERout$. The link graph is
therefore a random geometric graph \cite{penrose}: $N$ uniform points, joined
when closer than $r$. \texttt{VERIFICATION 5} confirms that the BER rule and
the distance rule give identical adjacency matrices for every graph of the
$J/S$ sweep.

\paragraph{The jammer's choice of $\rho$.}
Removing edges cannot increase $\lambda_2$: by \eqref{eq:qf}, fewer edges make
$\bx\T L\bx$ smaller for every $\bx$, hence also its minimum over
$\bx\perp\bone$. A smaller range removes edges, so the $\rho$ that minimises
$\lambda_2$ is the $\rho$ that minimises $r$. It is the same for every
placement and every $N$, and the script computes it once per $J/S$ value
(\texttt{worst\_rho()}). \texttt{VERIFICATION 4} checks the monotonicity
placement by placement; \texttt{VERIFICATION 6} checks that the $\rho$ with the
lowest mean $\lambda_2$ on the grid of the $\rho$-sweep is the grid neighbour
of the range-minimising $\rho$.

\paragraph{Which $\rho$ that is (figure \texttt{s2\_lambda2\_vs\_rho}).}
Minimising the range means bringing down the strongest possible link, which is
the question answered in Section~4 of the S1 note:
\[
  \rho_{\mathrm{out}} \approx \frac{\BERout}{\Q(\sqrt{x^\ast})} = 8.55\,\BERout = 0.0086 .
\]
The exact search gives $0.0091$ at $J/S_{\mathrm{ref}}=-14$\,dB (thermal noise
moves it slightly) and $\lambda_2$ is lowest at the nearest grid point,
$0.0089$. This is an order of magnitude below the $0.135$ of the S1 figure, but
for a simple reason: both are $\rho^\ast=0.709/(E_b/N_J)$, evaluated at
$E_b/N_J=8$\,dB in the S1 figure and at the outage point $19.2$\,dB here. It is
not a network effect, and it scales with the assumed criterion
($\BERout=10^{-2}$ would give $\rho_{\mathrm{out}}=0.086$).
The rise of $\lambda_2$ on the left of the figure has a separate cause: a
jammed hop has a BER of at most $\tfrac12$, so $P_b\le\rho/2$ plus the thermal
term, and no jammer power can bring a link down once $\rho<2\,\BERout$.

\paragraph{One-channel limit.}
$\rho_{\mathrm{out}}=0.0086$ is just below $1/N_{\mathrm{ch}}=0.01$, the
smallest fraction a jammer can occupy with 100 channels; the figure shades
that region. Enforcing $\rho\ge0.01$ changes the range at $-14$\,dB from
$538.8$\,m to $539.7$\,m and the thresholds below by $0.03$\,dB.

\section{The range--power relation and the collapse threshold}

\paragraph{Closed form.}
When jamming dominates thermal noise, the longest surviving link has
$E_b/N_J = 0.0829/\BERout$ (S1 note, Section~4). With
$E_b/N_J(d) = G_p - J/S_{\mathrm{ref}} - 10n\log_{10}(d/d_{\mathrm{ref}})$ in dB,
\begin{equation}
  J/S_{\mathrm{ref}}(r)\,[\mathrm{dB}] \;\approx\; G_p
  - 10\log_{10}\!\frac{0.0829}{\BERout}
  - 10\,n\log_{10}\!\frac{r}{d_{\mathrm{ref}}} .
  \label{eq:rp}
\end{equation}
Three consequences:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item every threshold moves by $+10$\,dB per tenfold increase of
        $N_{\mathrm{ch}}$ (as long as $\rho_{\mathrm{out}}\ge1/N_{\mathrm{ch}}$),
        and by $-10$\,dB per tenfold \emph{decrease} of $\BERout$;
  \item the \emph{spacing} between the curves for different $N$ is
        $10\,n\log_{10}(r_{N_1}/r_{N_2})$: independent of $\BERout$, $G_p$ and
        $d_{\mathrm{ref}}$, but proportional to the path-loss exponent $n$;
  \item the absolute values of $J/S_{\mathrm{ref}}$ carry no meaning beyond the
        chosen $d_{\mathrm{ref}}$; the geometry enters only through $r/L$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Collapse threshold (figure \texttt{s2\_lambda2\_vs\_js}).}
$J/S^\ast$ is defined as the $J/S_{\mathrm{ref}}$ at which half of the random
placements are still connected. The script sweeps $J/S_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in 1\,dB
steps over 500 placements per $N$, interpolates the 0.5 crossing linearly, and
obtains a 95\,\% interval by resampling the placements (bootstrap, 1000
resamples).

\midrule
10 & $-11.79$ & $-11.99\,..\,-11.61$ & $0.421\,L$ & $-11.68$ & $-17.35$ \\
20 & $-9.42$  & $-9.55\,..\,-9.29$   & $0.323\,L$ & $-9.36$  & $-17.22$ \\

Going from 10 to 20 nodes buys 2.4\,dB of jamming tolerance and from 20 to 30
another 1.5\,dB: the gain per added node shrinks, because the range needed to
keep $N$ random points connected shrinks more slowly as $N$ grows.
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rp} reproduces the simulated thresholds within 0.11\,dB
(\texttt{VERIFICATION 7}); the remainder is thermal noise. The knee, in
contrast, is almost the same for all three sizes ($-17.2$ to $-17.4$\,dB).

\paragraph{Limits of the model.}
Both results rest on the hard outage threshold without coding. With a coded
packet-error model $\rho_{\mathrm{out}}$ and the threshold values would change;
with a jammer at a finite distance the received $J$ would differ between nodes
and the graph would no longer be a plain random geometric graph. Both are
listed as extensions in the README.
