\noindent\textbf{Purpose.}
S0 is the calibration stage of the repository: the simulator must reproduce a
result whose exact answer is known before it is used on problems (S1, S2) that
have no closed form. This note derives that answer, maps every symbol to a line
of the code, and explains what the ``ratio $0.96$--$1.03$'' verification actually
proves. Mathematics used: Gaussian random variables, conditional probability,
the binomial estimator.

\section{Signal model}

One bit $b\in\{0,1\}$ is transmitted per symbol. Binary phase-shift keying maps it to
\[
  s = \begin{cases} +\sqrt{E_b} & b=1 \\ -\sqrt{E_b} & b=0 \end{cases}
\]
so that every symbol carries energy $s^2 = E_b$ (energy per bit).
The channel adds white Gaussian noise. After the receiver's matched filter the
decision variable is a single real number
\[
  r = s + n, \qquad n \sim \mathcal N\!\left(0,\ \sigma^2\right).
\]

\paragraph{Where $\sigma^2 = N_0/2$ comes from.}
$N_0$ is the \emph{one-sided} noise power spectral density (power per Hz of
positive frequency). A real matched filter for a unit-energy pulse has a noise
output variance equal to $N_0/2$ --- half of the density, because the two-sided
spectrum spreads $N_0$ over positive and negative frequencies at $N_0/2$ each.
Hence
\[
  \sigma^2 = \frac{N_0}{2}.
\]
\emph{This factor of two is the single most common source of a simulated BER
curve that is ``a few dB off'' from theory.}

\section{The decision rule and the error event}

Both symbols are equally likely, and the noise is symmetric, so the
maximum-likelihood decision is a threshold at zero:
\[
  \hat b = \begin{cases} 1 & r>0 \\ 0 & r\le 0. \end{cases}
\]
Condition on $b=1$ (so $s=+\sqrt{E_b}$). An error occurs when $r\le 0$, i.e.\ when
$n \le -\sqrt{E_b}$:
\[
  P(\text{error}\mid b=1) = P\!\left(n\le-\sqrt{E_b}\right)
  = P\!\left(\frac{n}{\sigma} \le -\frac{\sqrt{E_b}}{\sigma}\right)
  = \Q\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{E_b}}{\sigma}\right),
\]
where $\Q(x) = P(Z > x)$ for $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ is the Gaussian tail function and we
used the symmetry $P(Z\le -x)=P(Z\ge x)$. By the same argument
$P(\text{error}\mid b=0)$ is identical, so the average bit error rate is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BER} = \Q\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{E_b}}{\sigma}\right)
  = \Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{E_b}{N_0/2}}\right)
  = \boxed{\ \Q\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2E_b}{N_0}}\right)\ }.
  \label{eq:ber}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Stop here and check.}
Put $E_b=1$, $\EbNo = 10$ (10\,dB). Then $\sigma = \sqrt{1/20}=0.2236$ and the
argument of $\Q$ is $1/0.2236 = 4.47$. Confirm from a table or code that
$\Q(4.47)\approx 3.9\times10^{-6}$, which is the last point of the S0 curve.

\section{$\Q$ and $\erfc$}

The code uses \texttt{scipy.special.erfc} because SciPy does not ship $\Q$
directly. The two are related by
\[
  \Q(x) = \tfrac12\,\erfc\!\left(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt2}\right)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \mathrm{BER} = \tfrac12\,\erfc\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{E_b}{N_0}}\right),
\]
which is exactly \texttt{ber\_bpsk\_theory()} in the script.

\section{Correspondence with the code}

The script fixes $E_b = 1$, so $N_0 = 1/(\EbNo)$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{N_0/2}$.

\section{What the Monte Carlo estimate is, and how accurate}

The simulator observes $K$ errors in $n$ bits and reports $\widehat{\mathrm{BER}} = K/n$.
Each bit is an independent Bernoulli trial with success probability $p=\mathrm{BER}$,
so $K\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)$ and
\[
  \mathbb E[\widehat{\mathrm{BER}}] = p, \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}[\widehat{\mathrm{BER}}] = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}\approx\frac{p}{n}.
\]
The \emph{relative} standard deviation is therefore
\[
  \frac{\sqrt{p/n}}{p} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{np}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathbb E[K]}}.
\]
This is why the script stops each point after \texttt{MIN\_ERRORS = 200} errors
rather than after a fixed number of bits: 200 observed errors give a relative
spread of $1/\sqrt{200}\approx 7\%$ regardless of how small $p$ is. It also
explains why the low-BER points need far more bits (six million at 9\,dB versus
one million at 0\,dB): the required $n \approx 200/p$ grows as $p$ shrinks.

\paragraph{Why the pass band is $0.7 \le \text{ratio} \le 1.4$.}
With a 7\,\% relative spread, a ratio outside $[0.7,1.4]$ is roughly a
four-standard-deviation event --- essentially impossible by chance across ten
points. A ratio outside that band, or all ratios biased to one side, therefore
indicates a modelling error (usually $\sigma$), not statistical noise. The
observed range $0.957$--$1.025$ is what an unbiased estimator with $\sim$7\,\%
spread looks like.

\section{Why the curve is nearly a straight line on a log axis}

For large $x$ the Gaussian tail satisfies
\[
  \Q(x) \approx \frac{1}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-x^2/2},
\]
so with $x^2 = 2\EbNo$ the BER falls like $e^{-\EbNo}$: exponentially in the
\emph{linear} $\EbNo$. On a log-$y$ axis against $\EbNo$ in dB this is not exactly
straight, but close to it over the plotted range, and each additional dB buys
roughly a factor of 1.26 in $\EbNo$ and a much larger factor in BER at the
high-SNR end. This exponential decay is the benchmark that partial-band jamming
destroys in S1, where the decay becomes only inverse-linear.
